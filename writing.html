Philosophy of science · AI and institutions

Writing

These essays ask what happens when increasingly capable machines enter parts of science and white-collar cognition that were previously inseparable from human intellectual labor.

The papers are connected, but they ask different questions: what inquiry is for, who gets to direct research capacity, and what remains of human judgment and human worth when cognition becomes scalable.

Essay · automated science and understanding

When Ideas Become Expensive: What Science Is For When Calculation Becomes Cheap

Automated systems can produce valid results, verify them, understand them, and continue from them without thereby satisfying the human curiosity that gave scientific machinery its purpose. The essay follows natural philosophy through specialization and automation, then asks what happens when formal and experimental production outruns the human capacity to reconstruct the objects, dependencies, and conceptual relations that make those results part of a human scientific tradition.

The governing distinction is between replacing the means of inquiry and satisfying its end. Another subject's understanding can be genuine without automatically becoming ours.

Essay · automated science and allocation

Who Directs Automated Science? Inquiry, Allocation, and the Ownership of Research Capacity

Every research system chooses among more possible inquiries than it can materially realize. Automated R&D raises the downstream reach of those choices because one allocation decision can mobilize large amounts of model, compute, and laboratory capacity. The essay examines predictable exploration, the difficulty of funding inquiries whose destination is not yet legible, the difference between discovering the sea and owning the ship, and the case for plural access to frontier research machinery.

The institutional question is whether automated science only optimizes approved destinations or preserves the capacity to discover destinations no allocator could have specified beforehand.

Working essay · machine cognition and human agency

Judgment, warrant, and human value under scalable machine cognition

The usual response to cognitive automation is that humans will remain necessary because humans possess judgment. This draft decomposes that category. Context-sensitive execution can itself be structurally repetitive; constitutive judgment can also be performed by machines; and the human problem becomes maintaining an independent warrant boundary around systems whose reasoning we increasingly rely on. The economic argument then separates productive value, epistemic authority, and relational human value.
