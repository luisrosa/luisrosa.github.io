About

Physics, machine learning, and the structure of problems.

I study physics at the University of Michigan, with additional training in computer science and mathematics. My work has moved across theoretical machine learning, mechanistic interpretability, scientific ML, computational physics, and philosophy of science.

The recurring pattern is more stable than the domain. I tend to enter an underspecified problem, identify which assumptions are silently doing the work, separate concepts that have been collapsed together, and then formalize or engineer the resulting object far enough that its consequences can be tested.

Research and engineering

In industry, I have worked on machine-learning experimentation, fine-tuning, evaluation, deployment, and research tooling under practical compute constraints. In academic and independent work, I have studied neural architecture individuation, mechanistic localization, scientific inference, complex systems, and the relation between automated research and human inquiry.

I care about the boundary between a result and the evidence that licenses the result. That shows up differently in different projects: a representation may preserve information while making it inaccessible to a restricted continuation; a receiver may predict a phenomenon in one distribution without supporting the same rule in another; a visually plausible cell detection may still fall short of biological identity; a machine-generated proof may be correct without yet becoming part of a human scientific tradition.

Current direction

I am most interested in research at the boundary of ML foundations, model architecture, mechanistic interpretability, scientific ML, and AI-for-science. I am also interested in the governance problems created when automated systems become capable of performing more of the scientific and analytical work that institutions currently allocate to humans.
